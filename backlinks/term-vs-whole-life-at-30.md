I work as an independent insurance broker, and something I saw a few years ago has stuck with me.

A 30-year-old client in Columbus came to me with a whole life illustration folded into quarters. A captive agent had quoted him $612 a month for $500,000 of coverage. A 25-year term policy for the same death benefit was a small fraction of that price.

The usual pitch is that whole life is 'forced savings' and 'an investment.' But when you compare the cash value growth to what the price difference would earn if invested on its own, term plus investing wins for most people at that age. For everyone else the math points the other way. Curious whether anyone here has been pitched whole life and what the agent's argument was.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Is term life or whole life insurance better for a 30-year-old?

For most 30-year-olds, term life is the better choice, and it isn't especially close. I'm an independent broker, and I'd put it at about 95 percent of people in that age range.

Here is the core reasoning. Life insurance exists to replace your income if you die while people depend on it. At 30 that usually means a mortgage, maybe young kids, maybe a spouse who relies on your paycheck. Those obligations are largest now and shrink over time. Term insurance matches that: you buy a large death benefit for 20 or 30 years at a low, fixed premium.

Whole life bundles that protection with a savings component. The cost of that bundle is steep. I once sat with a 30-year-old who had been quoted $612 a month for $500,000 of whole life, while a 25-year term policy for the same amount cost a small fraction of that. The cash value inside the whole life policy grows slowly in the early years, and commissions on these products are typically much higher, which is why they're pitched so heavily.

If you take the premium difference and invest it yourself, in a retirement account or a low-cost index fund, you will usually end up well ahead, with more flexibility and no surrender penalties.

Where does whole life make sense? A dependent with special needs who will need lifelong support, estate liquidity for high net worth families, or certain business funding situations. If that's not you, it's likely not worth it.

Practical tips: buy the term length that covers your longest obligation, compare quotes from several carriers, and make sure any agent explains what happens to your cash value if you stop paying.
